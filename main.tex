\note{\textasciitilde 0:40. A quick reminder of NMA. If we have trials of A versus B and trials of B versus C, we can compare A with C indirectly through the common comparator B: d AC equals d AB plus d BC. Do that across a whole network and you can compare many treatments at once, as long as every treatment is joined by a path of randomised trials. On the right is the same idea on the outcome scale: the A to B step from the AB trials and the B to C step from the BC trials chain together through B to give A versus C. The key assumption is constancy of relative effects: the effect of A versus B is the same in every trial, whichever trial it was measured in. That's what lets us add the two effects together, and it holds as long as effect modifiers are balanced across the trials. But what if effect modifiers aren't balanced? That's where population adjustment comes in.}
\end{frame}

\statement{Any network size, any target population. \textcolor{bristolred}{But only for connected networks.}}
\vspace{2mm}
\note{\textasciitilde 1:00. If effect modifiers differ between trials, we need to adjust for them, and that's what ML-NMR does. For studies with IPD we fit an individual-level regression: a study baseline mu, prognostic effects beta-1, effect modifiers beta-2, and treatment effects gamma. For studies with only aggregate data, we take that same individual model and integrate it over the study's covariate distribution, so there's no aggregation bias. That gives population adjustment in networks of any size, with estimates in whatever target population the decision needs. The catch: until now it needed a connected network.}
\end{frame}

\note{\textasciitilde 1:15. So ML-NMR, like NMA, needs a path of randomised trials joining the treatments, like the triangle on the left. Comparisons across that gap are unanchored. Of 268 NICE appraisals from 2010 to 2018, 18 used population adjustment methods, and 16 of those were unanchored comparisons using MAIC. And in over half of them, the decision involved more than two treatments, but MAIC and STC can only compare one pair of studies at a time. They also only give estimates in the aggregate study's population, and give you no way to assess whether the assumption they rest on is plausible.}

\note{\textasciitilde 0:45. Here's the set-up, using the same disconnected network as before. Sub-network 1, in purple, has the reference treatment. That's usually placebo, but it doesn't have to be: any well-informed, stable treatment will do. Sub-network 2, in green, has no randomised path back to it, or it could be a single-arm study. Look at the linear predictor: relative effects within sub-network 2 still come from its own trials, as long as it has randomised comparisons, and the covariate effects are shared across the whole network. What's missing is mu j-prime, the study baseline: the adjusted outcome on the reference treatment in those disconnected studies, which they can't tell us because the reference isn't observed there. So that's the one thing we need to bring in from outside sub-network 2. We call this ML-NMR with bridging, ML-NMR-B, and there are two ways to do it.}

\note{\textasciitilde 0:45. Three things to take away. One: ML-NMR-B brings population adjustment to disconnected networks and single-arm studies. It builds on existing baseline-exchangeability models, like Béliveau and Thom, but adds adjustment at the individual level and a two-stage fit that keeps randomisation within each sub-network intact. Two: the random baseline bridge allows for the unobserved differences between studies that are almost always there, by carrying them through as uncertainty. Three: the assumption every unanchored comparison makes is now something you can judge, through tau-mu, overlap and predictive checks, with sensitivity analyses showing how much the bridge matters.
