\note{\textasciitilde 1:15. NMA pools randomised trials to compare many treatments, but only when there's a path of trials joining them, like the triangle on the left. Randomisation within each trial means differences in prognostic factors between trials cancel out. Health technology assessment committees increasingly see the other two pictures: a treatment that only comes from a single-arm study, or whole sub-networks with no common comparator between them. Comparisons across that gap are unanchored. Of 268 NICE appraisals from 2010 to 2018, 18 used population adjustment methods, and 16 of those were unanchored comparisons using MAIC. And in over half of them, the decision involved more than two treatments, but MAIC and STC can only compare one pair of studies at a time. They also only give estimates in the aggregate study's population, and give you no way to assess whether the assumption they rest on is plausible.}

\statement{Any network size, any target population. \textcolor{bristolred}{But only for connected networks.}}
\vspace{2mm}
\note{\textasciitilde 1:00. Quick recap of ML-NMR. For studies with IPD we fit an individual-level regression: a study baseline mu, prognostic effects beta-1, effect modifiers beta-2, and treatment effects gamma. For studies with only aggregate data, we take that same individual model and integrate it over the study's covariate distribution, so there's no aggregation bias. That gives population adjustment in networks of any size, with estimates in whatever target population the decision needs. The catch: until now it needed a connected network.}

\note{\textasciitilde 1:15. The random baseline bridge has two stages. Stage 1: fit ML-NMR to sub-network 1 with random effects on the study baselines. That gives a mean and a between-study SD, tau-mu, which is the variation left after adjusting for covariates. From that we get a predictive distribution for the baseline in a new study. Stage 2: use that as an informative prior on the baselines of the disconnected studies, and fit the whole network with ML-NMR. Doing it in two stages means we never share non-randomised information across the connected parts of the network, so randomisation within each sub-network is preserved. It assumes the adjusted baselines of all bridged studies are exchangeable, and because some unobserved differences between studies are almost always there, we prefer this bridge where the data allow.}

\note{\textasciitilde 0:45. The fixed baseline bridge is simpler. Pick one anchor study from each side and give them a single shared baseline, so after adjusting for the covariates they're modelled as one multi-arm study. It assumes the pair's adjusted baselines are identical, so it carries no extra uncertainty for unobserved differences. It's what you need when only one study informs the reference sub-network, so tau-mu can't be estimated, or when you can only justify exchangeability for one specific pair. The anchor can be an internal trial or an external single-arm or observational study. Where both bridges can be fitted, we use the random bridge as the main analysis and fixed bridges as sensitivity analyses.}
\end{frame}
